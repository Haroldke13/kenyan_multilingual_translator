===============================================================================
 LANGUAGES OF KENYA — a working list for kenyan_multilingual_translator
===============================================================================

WHY THIS FILE EXISTS
--------------------
language_ai/languages.py is the registry of what the app can actually do today.
This file is the wider map: every language spoken in Kenya, and — for each one —
whether adding it is an afternoon's work or a research project.

That distinction is the whole point. Adding a language to this app needs two
things:

  1. an NLLB-200 tag, so the translation model knows what it is reading
  2. an MMS adapter, if you want to accept speech as well as text

Where both exist, adding a language really is close to a download plus a row in
LANGUAGES. Where the NLLB tag does not exist, there is no shortcut: you need a
parallel corpus and a fine-tune, which is a different order of effort. Grouping
the list by that fact is more useful than grouping it alphabetically.

Kenya has roughly 60-70 living languages depending on how you count dialect
clusters — Luhya and Kalenjin and Mijikenda are each counted here as one entry
with their varieties listed, because that is how speakers and the census treat
them, even though Ethnologue splits them.


HOW TO READ THE STATUS COLUMN
-----------------------------
  LIVE      already in language_ai/languages.py and working
  READY     NLLB tag exists → text translation is reachable now
  SPEECH    MMS adapter exists but no NLLB tag → transcription only, no MT
  CORPUS    neither → needs a parallel corpus before anything works
  ?         could not confirm; verify before relying on it

⚠️ VERIFY BEFORE WIRING ANYTHING IN. ISO codes below are ISO 639-3 and are
reliable. NLLB tags and MMS adapter availability were checked in September 2026
against Meta's published language lists, but model coverage changes and a tag
that looks plausible may not exist. Confirm against the FLORES-200 language list
and the MMS supported-languages table before adding a row to LANGUAGES.
Speaker figures are rounded orders of magnitude from census and Ethnologue
estimates; treat them as "how big roughly", never as data.

  Swahili is the national language and co-official with English. Most Kenyans

  The Oromo entry deserves a note. NLLB's gaz_Latn is West Central Oromo, which
  is the Ethiopian variety. Borana Oromo as spoken in Marsabit and Isiolo is
  close but not identical, so output will be usable and slightly off. Worth
  labelling honestly in the UI rather than claiming Borana support.

  The last three are here because Kenya hosts large refugee and migrant
  communities at Kakuma and Dadaab and in Nairobi. They are not indigenous
  Kenyan languages, but they are languages spoken in Kenya by people who need
  translation, which is arguably the point of the app.

  CLUSTERS, EXPANDED

  Luhya varieties (each is a distinct ISO language; speakers usually say
  "Luhya"):  Bukusu (bxk) · Maragoli/Logooli (rag) · Idakho-Isukha-Tiriki (ida)
  Kabras · Khayo (lko) · Kisa (lks) · Marama (lrm) · Nyala (nle) · Nyore (nyd)
  Samia (lsm) · Tachoni (lts) · Tsotso · Wanga (lwg)

  Kalenjin varieties:  Kipsigis (sgc) · Nandi · Keiyo (eyo) · Marakwet (enb)
  Tugen (tuy) · Sabaot (spy) · Terik (tec) · Pökoot (pko, listed separately)

  Mijikenda varieties (coastal Bantu, ~2.0M combined, all CORPUS):
  Giryama (nyf) · Digo (dig) · Duruma (dug) · Chonyi (coh) · Rabai
  Ribe · Kambe · Jibana · Kauma

  A practical note on where to start. Luhya and Kalenjin have the most speakers
  and the least model coverage, so they are the highest-value targets — but they
  are clusters, and picking the wrong variety produces something that reads as
  wrong to most of the people you were trying to serve. Bukusu for Luhya and
  Kipsigis for Kalenjin are the usual pragmatic choices: each is the largest
  single variety in its cluster and each has the most written material.

  The Bible-alignment approach this project already uses works well here. Most
  of these languages have a complete Bible translation, which is the largest
  aligned parallel corpus that exists for any of them, and often the only one.

===============================================================================
 NOT A LANGUAGE FAMILY, BUT SPOKEN IN KENYA
===============================================================================
xki   Kenyan Sign Language (KSL)              ~0.6M deaf and hard of hearing

  Constitutionally recognised. Deliberately listed here because a project called
  a "multilingual translator" that ignores KSL is making a choice, and it should
  be a deliberate one. Sign language translation is a different technical
  problem — video in, not audio — and is out of scope for the current
  architecture. Saying so is better than quietly omitting it.

—     Sheng                                   widespread, urban youth

  A Swahili-English-ethnic-language urban vernacular, mainly Nairobi. Not a
  separate language by most definitions, but it is what a great many young
  Kenyans actually speak, it changes fast, and it will show up in any real user
  input. Worth handling as noise-tolerance in the Swahili path rather than as
  its own entry.

—     Kenyan Indian languages                 ~0.1M

  Gujarati (guj), Punjabi (pan), Hindi (hin), Konkani, Kutchi. All except Kutchi
  and Konkani have solid NLLB coverage, so they are technically READY if ever
  wanted.

—     Arabic                                  coastal and religious use
  arb_Arab — NLLB coverage is excellent. Relevant on the coast and in religious
  contexts.


===============================================================================
 SUGGESTED ORDER OF WORK
===============================================================================
1. Finish what is already reachable. Ganda, Kinyarwanda and Nuer have NLLB tags
   and serve real refugee and migrant populations in Kenya. Cheapest wins here.

2. Bukusu (Luhya) and Kipsigis (Kalenjin). Largest speaker populations with zero
   coverage anywhere. Highest impact per unit of effort, and the Bible-alignment
   method this repo already uses is the realistic route in.

3. Gusii and Meru. Both over two million speakers, both entirely unserved, both
   with complete Bible translations to align against.

4. Maasai and Turkana. Smaller, but both are spoken in regions where English and
   Swahili literacy is lowest, which is exactly where a translator is worth most.

5. Everything else, documentation-first for the endangered ones.
